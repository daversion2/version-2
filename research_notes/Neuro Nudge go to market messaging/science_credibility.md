## 1. aMCC: what the research shows, how strong it is, and whether "grow your aMCC by doing hard things" holds up

### Takeaway
The aMCC (anterior mid-cingulate cortex) is a real hub in the brain's salience and executive networks. Good reviews link it to estimating the cost and benefit of effort and to staying with hard tasks ("tenacity"). The evidence is correlational: activation during effort, and cortical thickness in a small group of superagers. **No human study found here shows that doing things you dislike makes the aMCC grow.** That idea comes from Huberman's 2023 podcast with Goggins and goes beyond the research. Saying the region is "linked to" effort and persistence is defensible. Saying an activity "grows", "strengthens" or "trains" it is not.

### Cited findings
- **The core review:** Touroutoglou, Andreano, Dickerson and Barrett, "The tenacious brain: How the anterior mid-cingulate contributes to achieving goals," *Cortex* (2020). It argues that the aMCC is a network hub that performs the cost/benefit computations needed for tenacity, and may forecast the energy needed for attention, memory encoding and movement. **[V]** — [PMC6884085](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6884085); [British Library record](https://eld.bl.uk/catalog/100149715011_0x000001)
- **The theory frame is allostasis, not willpower:** a companion paper by the same group, "Motivation in the Service of Allostasis: The Role of anterior Mid Cingulate Cortex," proposes that motivation anticipates the body's energy needs and meets them before they arise. It covers individual differences in aMCC processing, ranging from tenacity to apathy. **[V]** — [PMC7381101](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7381101)
- **The 2020 review's abstract** ties the work to successful aging and to neuropsychiatric conditions such as depression and dementia. **[V]** — [search summary of the Cortex review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6884085)
- **The superager evidence is correlational:** in Sun et al. (2016, *J Neurosci*), superagers' cortex was thicker than typical older adults' in salience- and default-network regions, including the aMCC, and in some regions it could not be told apart from young adults'. The sample was small and the design cross-sectional. **[V]** — [PMC5039247](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5039247); press coverage: [Harvard Gazette 2022](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2022/01/exploring-why-some-remain-sharp-even-as-decades-roll-by), [National Geographic](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/health/article/superagers-brain-personality-differences)
- **Touroutoglou in her own institution's blog:** she describes the aMCC as contributing to memory by sustaining effort on challenging tasks, framed as the question "is tenacity a matter of willpower or does brain structure play a role too?" **[V]** — [Mass General Research Institute blog, 2020](https://mgriblog.org/2020/09/30/is-tenacity-a-matter-of-willpower-or-does-brain-structure-play-a-role-too/)
- **The popular claim:** summaries of Huberman's episode with David Goggins (Jan 2023) say the aMCC "gets larger when people do things they don't want to do," that it is smaller in obese people and grows when they diet, and that it is "hyperplastic". Huberman's own AI Q&A softens this to "can *potentially* increase the size." **[V]** — [Huberman Lab AI answer](https://ai.hubermanlab.com/s/04QMEntw); [Medium summary](https://medium.com/scott-d-clary/discomfort-huberman-the-anterior-midcingulate-cortex-amc-5551b074caa8); [NAD.com summary](https://www.nad.com/news/dr-andrew-huberman-and-david-goggins-using-willpower-to-slow-brain-aging)
- **The critique:** a fact-check-style summary says imaging links the aMCC to cognitive effort and to resisting food cues, but that the claim dieting or effort physically enlarges the region (in volume or cortical thickness) is an extrapolation. This is a secondary source. **[V]** — [holdsup.app](https://holdsup.app/topic/anterior-midcingulate-cortex-plasticity)
- **The general guardrail is reverse inference:** concluding a mental state ("willpower") from activation in a region is weak, because most regions, the cingulate cortex included, activate for many processes. The dorsal cingulate is also linked to pain, conflict, error detection and negative affect. **[T]** — [Poldrack 2006, *TiCS*](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2005.12.004)
- **A related cost-of-control model:** Shenhav, Botvinick and Cohen's "Expected Value of Control" puts the dorsal ACC (the region now often called the aMCC) in charge of deciding whether control is worth its cost. This is the strongest mechanistic support for "your brain predicts the cost." **[T]** — [Shenhav et al. 2013, *Neuron*](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2013.07.007)

### Inferences
- **Defensible:** "Scientists have linked a brain region called the anterior mid-cingulate cortex to weighing effort against reward and to persisting on hard tasks. Some research finds it better preserved in older adults with unusually sharp memories."
- **Overreach (avoid):** "Grow your aMCC." "Every override builds your willpower muscle in your brain." "Neuro-Nudge strengthens your aMCC." "The willpower center of the brain." No intervention study supports these, and the app does not measure brain structure. The FTC has acted against exactly this kind of brain-benefit claim (see section 8).
- The researchers' own framing (allostasis: the brain budgeting energy) fits the app's thesis ("your brain predicts the cost and you feel it as resistance") better than the Huberman framing. Using Barrett's framing is both more accurate and more distinctive.
- Even if the region were trainable, the app's self-reports would not show it. "Friction felt going down" is a psychological measure, not a brain measure.

### Gaps
- No human longitudinal or intervention study showing that effortful practice increases aMCC thickness or volume turned up. The PMC full texts could not be fetched to check whether the reviews themselves cite any training effects.
- No public statement by Lisa Feldman Barrett on Huberman's use of her group's work was found. One may exist on social media, but it could not be confirmed.
- The claim that the aMCC is smaller in people with obesity and grows with dieting: no primary paper was located.

## 2. Learned industriousness, the effort paradox, effort as reward, the IKEA effect, and whether effort is aversive

### Takeaway
Two things are true together, and the app's message works best if it says both. (a) Mental effort is, on average, reliably unpleasant: a 2024 meta-analysis found a strong link between effort and negative feeling. (b) Effort can also be valued, and rewarding effort can make people seek it out: learned industriousness (Eisenberger 1992), with a solid 2022 PNAS replication involving roughly 1,600 participants. "Rewarding effort can make effort feel less costly over time" is defensible. "Hard things become fun" is not.

### Cited findings
- **Learned industriousness:** Eisenberger's review argues that rewarding high effort gives the sensation of effort secondary reward value, which lowers its aversiveness and increases later persistence. The evidence base was largely animal studies plus small human studies. **[T]** — [Eisenberger 1992, *Psychological Review* 99(2)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.99.2.248)
- **A modern, preregistered-scale test:** Clay, Mlynski, Korb, Goschke and Job (2022, *PNAS* 119(5) e2111785119) rewarded working-memory training contingent on effort. In Exp. 1 (n = 121) reward was tied to effort measured through cardiovascular signals. Exps. 2a–2e (n = 1,457, online) checked replication. Effort-contingent reward increased later preference for harder math tasks, even when participants knew no reward would follow. The authors conclude that people can learn to value effort, and that avoiding demanding tasks may reflect learning history. **[V]** — [PMC8812552](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8812552); [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/941492)
- **The effort paradox:** Inzlicht, Shenhav and Olivola argue that effort is costly and avoided, yet people also value activities *because* they are effortful (mountaineering, the IKEA effect, effort justification). Effort can therefore add value. **[T]** — [Inzlicht, Shenhav & Olivola 2018, *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* 22(4)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2018.01.007)
- **Effort is aversive on average:** David, Vassena and Bijleveld (2024), "The Unpleasantness of Thinking," *Psychological Bulletin* 150(9):1070–1093. This was a meta-analysis of 170 studies (358 tasks, 4,670 participants), all using the NASA-TLX. It found a strong positive link between mental effort and negative affect. Only 1 of 15 moderators mattered: effort felt somewhat less aversive in Asian samples. The authors call mental effort "inherently aversive." *Note: Inzlicht is not an author; the brief misattributes the paper.* **[V]** — [APA press release](https://www.apa.org/news/press/releases/2024/08/hurts-to-think); [NYU abstract](https://ifp.nyu.edu/2024/journal-article-abstracts/bul0000443/); [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/new-study-confirms-thinking-hard-feels-unpleasant/)
- **The meta-analysis has been challenged:** Grund, Roelle and Schmid (2026, *Psychological Bulletin*) argue the "inherently aversive" conclusion is premature, because the NASA-TLX effort item captures task difficulty rather than intensity of thought, and because most tasks were imposed rather than self-chosen. A separate "cautionary note" from Münster makes similar points. **[V]** — [NYU abstract bul0000484](https://ifp.nyu.edu/2026/journal-article-abstracts/bul0000484/); [Münster CRIS](https://cris-portal.uni-muenster.de/portal/en/publication/193115402)
- **Demand avoidance:** when given a choice, people tend to pick the lower-effort option. **[T]** — [Kool et al. 2010, *JEP: General*](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0020198)
- **The opportunity-cost account:** the feeling of effort signals the value of the next-best thing you could be doing, not a depleted fuel tank. **[T]** — [Kurzban et al. 2013, *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X12003196)
- **The IKEA effect:** people value things they built themselves more highly than identical pre-built items, but only when they complete the task. **[T]** — [Norton, Mochon & Ariely 2012, *J Consumer Psychology*](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcps.2011.08.002)
- **The effort heuristic:** people judge work as higher quality when they believe it took more effort. **[T]** — [Kruger et al. 2004, *JESP*](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-1031(03)00065-9)

### Inferences
- The app's "harder days count for more" has a real basis: rewarding *effort* rather than outcome is exactly the condition that produced learned industriousness in Clay et al. The app does this by weighting hard sessions more. This may be the single most defensible science-to-feature link the product has.
- The 2024 meta-analysis supports the app's starting premise, that resistance is real and normal and not a character flaw. Messaging can say "feeling resistance is normal: research finds hard mental effort generally feels unpleasant."
- Do not say "effort becomes enjoyable" or "you'll learn to love hard things." The evidence is that preference for demand shifts modestly, and mostly in lab tasks.
- Learned industriousness evidence covers lab cognitive tasks over short spans. Generalising to "workouts, cold plunges and dreaded tasks over months" is an inference.

### Gaps
- No field or app-based test of learned industriousness over weeks or months was found.
- Inzlicht's blog or Substack writing on effort as reward was not fetched.

## 3. Ego depletion and "willpower is a muscle": what is safe to say now

### Takeaway
The original "willpower is a limited resource that runs out" effect largely failed two large preregistered multi-lab replications (2016: d = 0.04; 2021: d = 0.06, with Bayes factors favouring the null about 4:1). "Willpower is a muscle that tires" should not be presented as science. "A muscle that strengthens with practice" (self-control training) is also weakly supported. Safer ground is habit and learning ("starting gets easier with repetition") and beliefs about willpower.

### Cited findings
- **Hagger et al. (2016) registered replication report:** 23 labs, N = 2,141, sequential-task paradigm. Pooled effect d = 0.04, 95% CI [−0.07, 0.15], which includes zero. **[V]** — [Hagger & Chatzisarantis 2016 PDF](https://nature.berkeley.edu/garbelottoat/wp-content/uploads/hagger-chatzisarantis-2016.pdf); [Tilburg record](https://research.tilburguniversity.edu/en/publications/a-multi-lab-pre-registered-replication-of-the-ego-depletion-effec/)
- **Vohs et al. (2021, *Psychological Science*):** run by proponents of ego depletion. 36 labs, N = 3,531. Confirmatory test not significant (d = 0.06), with Bayesian analysis favouring the null about 4:1. An exploratory analysis with no exclusions gave d = 0.08. **[V]** — [PubMed 34520296](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34520296/); [PDF](https://carlsonschool.umn.edu/sites/carlsonschool.umn.edu/files/2026-01/vohs-et-al-2021-psci-ego-depletion.pdf)
- **Commentary:** neither study settles the matter. Baumeister and Vohs had criticised the 2016 task choice. **[V]** — [Frontiers in Human Neuroscience opinion (2021)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8134656/)
- **An overview of the debate:** Friese et al. (2019) analyse arguments for and against the effect being real. Inzlicht and Friese (2019) note that ego depletion has been "dethroned" as a model of self-control. **[T]** — [Friese et al. 2019, *PSPR*](https://doi.org/10.1177/1088868318762183); [Inzlicht & Friese 2019, *Social Psychology*](https://doi.org/10.1027/1864-9335/a000398)
- **Beliefs about willpower:** people who believed willpower is *not* easily depleted showed less of a depletion-like drop. **[T]** — [Job, Dweck & Walton 2010, *Psychological Science*](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797610384745)
- **Opportunity-cost alternative:** the apparent fatigue reflects shifting motivation and priorities, not a fuel shortage. **[T]** — [Kurzban et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X12003196)

### Inferences
- **Safe:** "The old idea that willpower is a tank that runs dry hasn't held up well in large replication studies." This is credible and contrarian, and it fits the product's voice.
- **Safe:** "Starting tends to get easier the more you repeat something" (habit; see section 4). This gives the app's "override strengthens with practice" a defensible basis without the muscle metaphor.
- **Avoid:** "Train your willpower muscle," "build self-control capacity," "deplete/recharge your willpower." If the muscle metaphor is used, flag it as a metaphor ("like a skill you practise").
- The app's "override" language is fine as product vocabulary. Just don't attach a biological mechanism to it.

### Gaps
- Meta-analyses of self-control training interventions were not retrieved. In training knowledge, their effects shrink after correcting for publication bias (Friese et al. 2017), but that is unverified here, so do not cite it.

## 4. Habit formation, and whether *starting* is what becomes automatic

### Takeaway
This is the app's strongest scientific ground. Habit researchers distinguish **habitual instigation** (the cue-triggered decision to *start*) from **habitual execution** (running through the steps). Instigation habit predicts how often people actually do a behaviour, including exercise, better than execution habit does. "How hard was it to start?" therefore measures roughly the thing habit science says should get easier with repetition. Automaticity builds gradually and takes much longer than 21 days, with wide individual variation (median about 66 days in Lally 2010).

### Cited findings
- **Instigation vs execution:** Gardner, Phillips and Judah (2016, *British Journal of Health Psychology* 21:613–630). With 229 students across flossing, snacking and breakfast, instigation habit predicted later behaviour frequency better than execution habit did. They suggest that breaking a habit may depend on overriding the habit of *starting* it. (Lally is not an author; the brief's "Gardner & Lally" attribution is loose.) **[V]** — [bepress abstract](https://works.bepress.com/lalison-phillips/3); [KCL repository](https://kclpure.kcl.ac.uk/portal/en/publications/21043ddd-56c3-4d6c-9a48-cf4fb0e47716)
- **Exercise specifically:** Phillips and Gardner (2016, *Health Psychology*), N = 123 adults. Instigation habit was the only unique predictor of exercise frequency. **[V]** — [Iowa State repository](https://dr.lib.iastate.edu/entities/publication/471bb77e-1678-449c-8004-f7547c47d2fa/full)
- **A later refinement:** Gardner, Rebar and Lally (2020, reply to Hagger) argue that physical-activity research has focused on instigation and neglected execution. This is a reminder that complex behaviours like workouts may stay effortful to *carry out* even once *starting* is automatic. **[V]** — [UCL Discovery](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10075588/)
- **The 66 days:** Lally, van Jaarsveld, Potts and Wardle (2010, *European Journal of Social Psychology* 40(6):998–1009). 96 volunteers chose an eating, drinking or activity behaviour in a consistent context. Automaticity rose along an asymptotic curve. The median time to reach 95% of each person's plateau was **66 days, range 18–254**. **Missing one opportunity did not materially affect the process.** Complex behaviours such as exercise plateaued more slowly. Small sample, with many fitted curves of poor quality. **[T]** — [doi:10.1002/ejsp.674](https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674)
- **A newer synthesis:** a 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis of health-habit formation reports median times of roughly 59–66 days, with ranges from days to almost a year. **[T]** — [Singh et al. 2024, *Healthcare* 12(23):2488](https://doi.org/10.3390/healthcare12232488)
- **What habit is:** habits are context-cued response dispositions learned through repetition. Once formed, they run with little dependence on current goals or intentions. **[T]** — [Wood & Neal 2007, *Psychological Review*](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.114.4.843)
- **Making habits the target of advice:** habit as a practical health tool (repeat in the same context, and automaticity follows). **[T]** — [Gardner, Lally & Wardle 2012, *BJGP*](https://doi.org/10.3399/bjgp12X659466); review: [Gardner 2015, *Health Psychology Review*](https://doi.org/10.1080/17437199.2013.876238)

### Inferences
- **Defensible core thesis:** "Habit researchers have found that what becomes automatic first is usually the *starting*, the decision to begin. That's why we ask how hard it was to start, separately from how hard it was to keep going."
- **Defensible:** "Research suggests that, on average, building a habit takes about two months, and that missing a day doesn't undo your progress." This directly supports a non-punitive approach to streaks.
- The split between "start" and "keep going" maps neatly onto instigation vs execution. A falling "start" score with a flat "keep going" score is the pattern habit theory predicts for hard activities like workouts or cold plunges. Messaging can present this honestly ("the first part to get easier is usually starting").
- **Caution:** "friction felt going down" could also reflect easier sessions, mood, response shift, regression to the mean, or users dropping hard tasks. The app cannot claim that a falling score *proves* the user is "getting stronger." Frame it as "your own record of how hard it felt."

### Gaps
- No study was found that uses a single "how hard was it to start" rating as a validated measure of instigation habit. The standard measure is the SRBAI / Self-Report Habit Index. The app's 1–3 scale is not validated.

## 5. Self-monitoring, implementation intentions, self-compassion after lapses, the what-the-hell effect, and streaks

### Takeaway
Monitoring progress works (moderate effect, stronger when recorded and visible). If-then plans work (medium to large effect across about 100 tests). Self-compassion after a lapse increases the motivation to improve and reduces procrastination. Streaks are double-edged: an intact streak shown in a log increases engagement, but a broken one decreases it, especially when users blame themselves. Letting users repair a streak reduces the damage. This supports the app's choice of difficulty-weighted totals over streaks.

### Cited findings
- **Self-monitoring:** Harkin et al. (2016, *Psychological Bulletin*), a meta-analysis of 138 studies (N ≈ 19,951). Prompting progress monitoring improved goal attainment (d ≈ 0.40). Effects were larger when progress was physically recorded or made public. **[T]** — [doi:10.1037/bul0000025](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000025)
- **Implementation intentions:** Gollwitzer and Sheeran (2006), a meta-analysis of 94 independent tests. If-then plans had a medium-to-large effect on goal attainment (d = 0.65), including on *getting started*. **[T]** — [doi:10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1)
- **Self-compassion after failure:** Breines and Chen (2012, *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin*), 4 experiments. Self-compassion after a failure or moral transgression *increased* motivation to improve, effort, and study time for a retest, compared with self-esteem boosting or no intervention. **[T]** — [doi:10.1177/0146167212445599](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167212445599)
- **Procrastination:** Sirois (2014) found that lower self-compassion is linked to more procrastination, partly through stress. **[T]** — [doi:10.1080/15298868.2013.763404](https://doi.org/10.1080/15298868.2013.763404). Students who forgave themselves for procrastinating on a first exam procrastinated less before the next one. **[T]** — [Wohl, Pychyl & Bennett 2010, *Personality and Individual Differences*](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.paid.2010.01.029)
- **Self-compassion and health behaviour:** a meta-analysis found self-compassion linked to better health-promoting behaviours. **[T]** — [Sirois, Kitner & Hirsch 2015, *Health Psychology*](https://doi.org/10.1037/hea0000158)
- **The what-the-hell / abstinence-violation pattern:** restrained eaters who had "blown" their diet with a preload then ate *more*. This is the classic counter-regulation finding behind the "what-the-hell effect." **[T]** — [Herman & Mack 1975, *Journal of Personality*](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-6494.1975.tb00727.x). The "abstinence violation effect" comes from Marlatt and Gordon's relapse-prevention model (1985, book; no link).
- **Streaks:** Silverman and Barasch (2023, *Journal of Consumer Research* 49(6):1095–1117), 7 studies. Intact streaks highlighted in logs increase later engagement relative to broken streaks. The effect grows when people blame themselves for the break and shrinks when they can repair it. People will pay costs, such as watching an ad, to repair a streak. **[V]** — [CU Boulder summary](https://www.colorado.edu/business/faculty-research/2023/04/19/or-track-how-broken-streaks-affect-consumer-decisions); [UDaily](https://udel.edu/udaily/2024/march/power-of-streaks-motivation-jackie-silverman); [Psychology Today](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/ulterior-motives/202306/how-broken-streaks-sap-motivation)
- **One missed day doesn't derail habit formation** (Lally 2010, section 4). **[T]** — [doi:10.1002/ejsp.674](https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674)
- **Fresh starts:** temporal landmarks (a new week or month, a birthday) increase goal-directed behaviour, which offers a way back after a lapse. **[T]** — [Dai, Milkman & Riis 2014, *Management Science*](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2014.1901)

### Inferences
- **Defensible:** "Tracking your progress is one of the better-supported ways to stick with a goal."
- **Defensible:** "Being kind to yourself after a slip isn't going soft. Studies find it makes people more likely to try again." This fits the comeback/lapse features.
- **Defensible design rationale:** "We don't punish broken streaks. Research shows a broken streak can make people give up entirely, and a single missed day doesn't undo a habit."
- If the app shows any streak, offer a repair or "keep it going" mechanic and attribute breaks to circumstances, not the user. Both moderators came out of Silverman & Barasch.

### Gaps
- Recent preregistered replications of implementation-intention effects (some suggest smaller effects in field settings) were not retrieved.
- No study comparing difficulty-weighted totals with streak counters was found.

## 6. Predicting the cost of effort, and "dread vs reality" (affective forecasting)

### Takeaway
"Your brain predicts the cost before you start" is well grounded in computational accounts of cognitive control (Expected Value of Control, opportunity cost). "People overestimate how bad things will feel" (impact bias) is robust for emotional *events*, but **not consistent for ordinary work tasks**. One study found no systematic over-forecasting of task aversiveness. The "dread vs reality" feature is better framed as "check your prediction against what happened," which is the core idea of exposure therapy (expectancy violation), than as "it's always less bad than you think."

### Cited findings
- **Expected Value of Control:** the brain (dorsal ACC) decides how much control to apply by weighing expected payoff against the inherent cost of effort. **[T]** — [Shenhav, Botvinick & Cohen 2013, *Neuron*](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2013.07.007)
- **The aMCC as an energy forecaster** (Touroutoglou et al. 2020; see section 1). **[V]** — [PMC6884085](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6884085)
- **Impact bias:** people overestimate how intense and long-lasting their emotional reactions to future events will be. **[T]** — [Wilson & Gilbert 2005, *Current Directions in Psychological Science*](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.0963-7214.2005.00355.x); [Gilbert et al. 1998, *JPSP*](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.75.3.617)
- **Supporting:** a 2023 VR pilot (n = 30) found participants predicted more intense negative (and positive) reactions than they reported after exposure. **[V]** — [Scientific Reports 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-36346-3)
- **Contradicting, for tasks:** a PLOS ONE study of work and study tasks found no systematic tendency to forecast tasks as more or less aversive than they turned out to be, and absolute errors were small. **[V]** — [PLOS ONE 2020](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0235973)
- **Mixed, for cognitive tasks:** in a study of 551 students, anticipated effort and discomfort did not match experience, and people often *underestimated* the demands of sustained-attention tasks. **[V]** — [Academia.edu record](https://www.academia.edu/79830958/Anticipated_experienced_and_remembered_subjective_effort_and_discomfort_on_sustained_attention_versus_working_memory_tasks)
- **Anticipation colours memory:** expecting to repeat an unpleasant experience makes it feel worse in retrospect (bracing). **[V]** — [APA, *JEP: General* 140(1)](https://www.apa.org/pubs/journals/releases/xge-140-1-63.pdf)
- **Worry predictions:** in GAD treatment, about 91% of tracked worry predictions did not come true. This was a small clinical sample. **[T]** — [LaFreniere & Newman 2020, *Behavior Therapy*](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.beth.2019.07.003)
- **Expectancy violation in exposure therapy:** the mismatch between the predicted outcome and the actual outcome drives learning that reduces fear and avoidance. **[T]** — [Craske et al. 2014, *Behaviour Research and Therapy*](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.brat.2014.04.006)

### Inferences
- **Defensible:** "Before something hard, your brain makes a prediction about how costly it will be. Recording what actually happened lets you check that prediction." This is accurate and doesn't promise the gap always runs one way.
- **Defensible, with hedge:** "For many people, the dread before a task is worse than the task itself. Tracking both shows you whether that's true for you."
- **Avoid:** "Things are always easier than you expect," "studies prove dread is worse than reality." That is contradicted for ordinary tasks.
- The app's own data (dread rating vs. experienced rating) could become a first-party insight ("in your history, starting felt harder than keeping going X% of the time"), which is honest and personal.

### Gaps
- No large study was found that directly compares anticipated with experienced aversiveness for *dreaded personal tasks* or procrastinated chores.

## 7. Cold exposure: what is defensible

### Takeaway
Cold-water immersion causes a large, acute rise in plasma catecholamines: noradrenaline +530% and dopamine +250% at 14 °C for one hour (Šrámek 2000, 10 young men). That measures **blood, not brain**, so it does not show a "dopamine boost" in the reward sense, and it was not measured afterwards for hours. Søberg 2021 shows thermoregulatory differences in a small group of habitual winter swimmers, which is cross-sectional and not causal. Defensible: "cold water is a reliable way to practise doing something your body resists." Not defensible: mood, dopamine, metabolism, immunity or resilience *benefits*.

### Cited findings
- **Šrámek et al. (2000, *European Journal of Applied Physiology* 81(5):436–442):** 1-hour head-out immersion at 32, 20 and 14 °C. At 14 °C, plasma noradrenaline rose 530% and dopamine 250%, metabolic rate rose 350%, and adrenaline did not change. The authors attribute the cold responses to sympathetic activation. **[V]** — [PubMed 10751106](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10751106/)
- **Popular distortion:** coverage adds a "two-hour dopamine high," but the paper has no two-hour post-immersion dopamine series. Plasma dopamine is also not a measure of synaptic dopamine release in the brain. **[V]** — [ScienceBlog](https://scienceblog.com/?p=599433)
- **Søberg et al. (2021, *Cell Reports Medicine*):** young male winter swimmers who combined cold dips and sauna, compared with controls. The swimmers had greater cold-induced heat production. At comfortable temperatures, brown fat was active in controls but not in the swimmers. Small, men-only, not causal, with possible confounding from lifestyle or genetics. **[V]** — [PubMed 34755128](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34755128/); [University of Copenhagen release](https://cbmr.ku.dk/news/2021/winter-swimming-scandinavian-men-can-teach-us-how-the-body-adapts-to-extreme-heat-and-cold)
- **Reviews:** cold-water immersion carries real risks (cold shock, arrhythmia, drowning). Claimed health benefits rest mostly on small, uncontrolled or cross-sectional studies. **[T]** — [Tipton et al. 2017, *Experimental Physiology*, "Cold water immersion: kill or cure?"](https://doi.org/10.1113/EP086283); [Espeland et al. 2022 systematic review, *Int J Circumpolar Health*](https://doi.org/10.1080/22423982.2022.2111789)
- **The one sizeable RCT:** cold showers (n ≈ 3,000, Netherlands) were associated with 29% fewer sickness-absence days, but **not** fewer illness days. **[T]** — [Buijze et al. 2016, *PLOS ONE*](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0161749)

### Inferences
- **Defensible:** "A cold plunge is a clean test of the moment your body says no and you go anyway." This is a statement about the experience, not a health benefit.
- **Defensible, with hedge:** "Cold water triggers a large, measurable stress response in the body."
- **Avoid:** "Boosts dopamine 250%," "burns fat," "strengthens immunity," "builds mental resilience." Under the FTC's 2022 standard (section 8) these would need RCT-grade evidence the app does not have.
- Add a safety line on any cold-plunge content (heart conditions, never alone in open water). This also fits Apple 1.4.1's "check with a doctor" expectation.

### Gaps
- No RCT evidence was found that cold exposure improves psychological resilience, discipline, or the ability to persist on other tasks.

## 8. Claims guardrails: FTC, Apple, FDA, and how to phrase neuroscience

### Takeaway
Under the FTC's 2022 Health Products Compliance Guidance, any health benefit claim, explicit *or implied*, generally needs "competent and reliable scientific evidence," usually randomized controlled human trials of the product itself or an essentially equivalent one. Citing studies of a *mechanism* (aMCC, dopamine) does not substantiate a claim that *the app* produces that effect. The FTC's 2016 Lumosity settlement over "brain training" claims is the direct precedent. Apple requires accurate metadata and extra scrutiny for health apps. The safe zone is general wellness, self-tracking, and attributed descriptions of research ("studies link…"), not efficacy claims about the app.

### Cited findings
- **FTC Health Products Compliance Guidance (Dec 2022):** replaced the 1998 supplement guide and applies to all health-related claims. Health-benefit claims generally require randomized, controlled human clinical testing. Quality counts more than quantity, and replication adds weight. Expected design features include a control group, randomization and blinding. Epidemiological evidence is generally not enough. It is business guidance and does not have the force of law. **[V]** — [FTC guidance PDF](https://ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/Health-Products-Compliance-Guidance.pdf); [Covington summary](https://www.cov.com/en/news-and-insights/insights/2023/01/ftc-issues-new-guidance-on-health-related-claims-to-replace-the-dietary-supplements-advertising-guide); [Cooley summary](https://www.cooley.com/news/insight/2023/2023-03-02-ftc-revises-health-products-compliance-guidance)
- **Precedent:** Lumos Labs (Lumosity) paid $2 million in 2016 to settle FTC charges that it deceptively claimed its games improved everyday performance and delayed age-related cognitive decline without adequate evidence. **[T]** — [FTC press release, Jan 2016](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2016/01/lumosity-pay-2-million-settle-ftc-deceptive-advertising-charges-its-brain-training-program)
- **Apple App Store Review Guideline 1.4.1:** medical apps that could give inaccurate data or information, or that could be used to diagnose or treat, may get greater scrutiny. Apps must disclose data and methodology behind accuracy claims and should remind users to check with a doctor before medical decisions. **[V]** — [App Store Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- **Apple 2.3 / 2.3.1(a):** metadata, screenshots and descriptions must accurately reflect the app's core experience. Misleading marketing, inside or outside the store, is grounds for removal. **Apple 1.1.6** bans false information and features, and "for entertainment purposes" disclaimers don't overcome it. **Apple 5.1.3** limits the use of health and fitness data. **[V]** — [App Store Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- **FDA General Wellness policy:** low-risk products intended for general wellness (e.g., maintaining healthy habits, managing stress) that make no disease claims are outside FDA device enforcement. **[T]** — [FDA, General Wellness: Policy for Low Risk Devices](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/general-wellness-policy-low-risk-devices)
- **Neuro-language persuades beyond its evidence:** adding irrelevant neuroscience made poor explanations more convincing to non-experts. **[T]** — [Weisberg et al. 2008, *J Cognitive Neuroscience*](https://doi.org/10.1162/jocn.2008.20040). Note: the related "brain images are persuasive" finding (McCabe & Castel 2008) replicated weakly. **[T]** — [Farah & Hook 2013, *Perspectives on Psychological Science*](https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691612469035)
- **Reverse inference** (activation ≠ mental state). **[T]** — [Poldrack 2006](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2005.12.004)

### Inferences — phrasing rules for the copy team
1. **Attribute, don't promise.** Use "Research links the aMCC to persistence," not "Neuro-Nudge builds your aMCC." The first describes the literature. The second is a product efficacy claim that needs product RCTs.
2. **Describe the user's own data, not their brain.** "Watch how hard things *feel* to you change over time" is safe. "Proof your brain is getting stronger" is not.
3. **No cognitive, mental-health or disease outcomes.** Avoid "reduces anxiety," "treats procrastination," "slows brain aging" (a superagers implication), "boosts dopamine," "burns fat." This keeps the app inside FDA general wellness and outside FTC health-claim territory.
4. **Hedge to match the evidence:** "linked to," "studies suggest," "on average," "for many people." Not "proven," "rewires," "grows."
5. **Metaphors labelled as metaphors.** "Like a muscle" is fine. "Your willpower muscle" stated as fact is not, because ego depletion did not replicate.
6. **Keep a citation page.** A "The science" screen or page listing the primary sources, with years, is credible and shows Apple reviewers the methodology behind any claim (1.4.1 / 2.3).
7. **Implied claims count.** Under FTC guidance, juxtaposing "aMCC grows with hard things" next to "Neuro-Nudge tracks hard things" implies the app grows the aMCC, even if neither sentence says so.

### Gaps
- The FTC PDF was not read directly; the language comes from law-firm summaries. Check exact wording before legal review.
- No FTC action against a *habit-tracking* app specifically was found. Lumosity (brain training) is the nearest precedent.
- UK and EU rules (ASA/CAP code, EU health-claims rules) were not covered and should be checked if the app markets there.
