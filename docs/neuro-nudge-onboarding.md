## Screen 6 — The Mechanism of Recovery
 
**Headline:**
> Dopamine doesn't just create craving. It also fuels focus and self-control.
 
**Body:**
> The same chemical driving that scroll is also what powers your prefrontal cortex — the part of your brain responsible for decisions, focus, and self-control. Chronic overstimulation is linked to weaker top-down control from this region, shifting behavior toward the reactive, impulsive parts of your brain. It's not permanent. The prefrontal cortex responds to training.
>
> The tool is deliberate discomfort — and it's not a new idea. Over 2,000 years ago, the Stoics practiced voluntary hardship on purpose: going without, sitting with discomfort, choosing the hard thing over the easy one, so they'd never be at the mercy of either. Same principle. When you voluntarily do something hard — sit in silence, hold a cold plunge, go for a walk without your phone — and you don't quit, you're practicing exactly the kind of self-regulation this circuitry runs on. Distress tolerance builds. Over time, your baseline shifts.
 
**CTA:** This is what the research shows →
 
> *Editor's note:* Added the dopamine→PFC bridge up top so Screen 3 and Screen 6 feel like one throughline instead of two separate topics — dopamine fuels craving *and* fuels the regulatory system that reins craving in, which is accurate (mesocortical dopamine pathway) and does the transition work for you. For Stoicism: framed as "over 2,000 years ago" rather than "a thousand" — Stoicism runs from ~300 BCE, and Seneca specifically wrote about deliberately practicing hardship, so this is the accurate window, not an undersell. Kept the rest of the "weaker top-down control" hedge from before since that's still the honest framing of that research.

## Screen 7 — The Science, Straight
 
**Headline:**
> This isn't just a mindset thing. There's real neuroscience underneath it.
 
**Body:**
> Cold water immersion has been shown to raise plasma dopamine by roughly 250% in controlled studies — a real, sustained increase, not a spike-and-crash.
>
> Meditation and cold exposure work through different pathways, but both are linked to better regulation between the brain's impulse-driven and decision-making systems.
>
> Regular meditators show differences in brain connectivity between regions tied to emotional reactivity and executive control — and these patterns appear to be trainable, not fixed.
>
> Many people who stick with practices like these report improvements in mood stability, focus, and motivation within a few weeks.
 
**CTA:** So what do you actually do? →
 
> *Editor's note — this screen had the most to fix:*
> - The 250% dopamine figure is real (Šrámek et al., cold water immersion), but it's specific to that study's conditions — sustained immersion, not a quick cold shower — so I scoped the claim to "cold water immersion" rather than "cold exposure" generally, and dropped "without dependence or crash" (true-sounding, but not something that study actually measured).
> - "Meditation and cold exposure produce overlapping changes in brain activity... same destination" was the shakiest line on the page — it asserts the two practices are neurally equivalent, which isn't established. Rewrote to say they work differently but share a *functional* outcome (better regulation), which is the defensible version of the claim.
> - "People who regularly practice distress tolerance" → "regular meditators" — the connectivity research this is drawing on is mostly in meditation/mindfulness literature specifically, not "distress tolerance" as a general category. Narrowed it to what's actually supported.
> - "Most people report... within two to four weeks" → "Many people... within a few weeks" — softened from a specific stat that reads like a clinical result to an honest, still-motivating claim.
