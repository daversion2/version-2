Why it’s so hard

It’s not a lack of willpower.
It’s a prediction.

Before anything hard, your brain predicts the cost, and you feel it as resistance. Acting anyway is called an override, and like any skill, it gets stronger with practice.

What you track

Workouts, meals, meditation and more.

The everyday things you already know you should do, all in one place.

What you train

Your override.

The skill of doing it when you’d rather not, measured with one honest question: how hard was it to start?

The science

A prediction, not a flawBefore anything hard, your brain forecasts what it will cost. You feel that forecast as resistance.
The effort regionDoing what you’d rather avoid engages the anterior mid-cingulate cortex, a region studies link to tenacity and willpower.
Effort can be learnedIn “learned industriousness” research, people rewarded for working hard come to find effort itself less aversive.
Why: People don’t download a concept, they download relief from a feeling. This section converts “I keep failing” (shame) into “my brain makes a prediction I can train” (hope). Naming it once, plainly, is enough; “override” now arrives as the answer, not as jargon the visitor has to learn first. The two cards show the product’s two layers side by side: the apps they expect (track) and the thing no one else offers (train). It sells the full range without a swipe at anyone else’s product. The science row sits directly under it, so the “prediction” idea is backed the moment it’s made. Wording is copied from the app’s own “Why this works” text, hedges included (“studies link”).

How it works

Two taps a session.
That’s the whole system.

No logging marathons. Rate the moment, do the thing, rate it again.

1

Before you start: how hard is it to begin?

The voice says “I’ll do it tomorrow.” You tap an honest answer, then go anyway.

The science: starting when you’d rather not is practice at acting against your brain’s prediction.

2

When you finish: how hard was it to keep going?

The voice says “this sucks, stop.” You tap how loud it got, and that you stayed.

The science: the hard last minutes are where the effort region works hardest.

A 3 isn’t a bad day. It’s the most training a session can give.
Why: Features are only persuasive once the visitor can picture themselves doing them. “Two taps” shrinks the perceived effort, which is the biggest drop-off risk for a self-improvement app. The phones stay (they are your proof that it’s real); the copy around them now says what the user does and feels, not what the screen contains. Each phone also gets one “science” line, so the screen and the brain mechanism are connected at the moment of looking.
